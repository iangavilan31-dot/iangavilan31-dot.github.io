Biography

A life shaped by virtuosity, migration, and musical dialogue

Ilmar Gavilán is a Cuban-American violinist born in Havana whose performing career has taken him across North America, Europe, and beyond. Public biographies from Manhattan School of Music and the Harlem Quartet describe a path that began with early recognition in Cuba and expanded through major international study and performance opportunities.

As a teenager, Gavilán left Cuba to continue advanced violin study at the Tchaikovsky Conservatory in Moscow. His training later took him to the Reina Sofía School of Music in Spain, where he studied with Zakhar Bron, and then to New York, where he studied at the Manhattan School of Music with Glenn Dicterow. He also earned a Graduate Diploma in Quartet Performance from New England Conservatory and completed a Doctor of Musical Arts degree at Rutgers University under Arnold Steinhardt.

As a soloist, he has appeared with ensembles including the Atlanta, Baltimore, Detroit, Milwaukee, St. Louis, Hartford, Nashville, Phoenix, Denver, Santa Fe, Havana, Mexico City, and Venezuela symphonies. His chamber career has been equally expansive, with collaborations involving Itzhak Perlman, Ida Kavafian, Carter Brey, Paul Katz, Fred Sherry, Anthony McGill, and Misha Dichter, as well as festival appearances at Tanglewood, Ravinia, and Angel Fire.

Gavilán is also central to the artistic identity of the Harlem Quartet, the ensemble he helped found through the Sphinx Organization. Alongside his classical work, he has collaborated in jazz and Afro-Cuban contexts with Chick Corea, Gary Burton, Paquito D'Rivera, Eddie Palmieri, and Dafnis Prieto. His career also includes extensive teaching, with public roles at Manhattan School of Music, Montclair State University, the Royal College of Music residency with Harlem Quartet, and earlier work at Juilliard's Music Advancement Program.

The documentary Los Hermanos/The Brothers brought wider public attention to his family story and to his artistic relationship with his brother, pianist and composer Aldo López-Gavilán. Together, their story frames Ilmar's work not only as performance, but as part of a larger conversation about family, migration, culture, and musical exchange.
