Impossible Lab

Four strange worlds. Turn channels, tune a colony, grow a garden, and survive a moving relay.

the curiositythe buildthe iterationthe practice

01 / the question behind it

Could four worlds with different rules become one connected experiment?

Impossible Lab combines a first-person Three.js world with a React interface, a field journal and local saves. Four rooms introduce different creatures and instruments, then ask the player to carry discoveries into physical hybrid experiments.

02 / from idea to something playable

how the pieces came together.

  1. 01

    Give each room one rule to learn

    The rooms turn channels, tune a swimming colony, divide a conserved water supply and test a moving relay. Each instrument makes its state visible instead of relying on a long instruction screen.

  2. 02

    Build creatures that communicate

    Creatures use original procedural geometry and articulated rigs. Their poses, hazards and actions share a clock, so anticipation and response belong to the same readable sequence.

  3. 03

    Connect discoveries across rooms

    The field campaign adds transfer plates, linked colonies, water gardens and a six-seal relay. Three physical hybrid proofs turn the collected discoveries into things the player can test in the world.

  4. 04

    Keep a complete journey

    The journal records observations, local saves preserve progress and the reactor provides an ending. Earlier saves keep their prior ending while new games use the expanded field campaign.

03 / the skills in the work

what this helped me practice.

The project develops procedural 3D modeling, articulated animation and the design of systems that explain themselves through play. It also connects interface writing, camera and collision work with persistent puzzle progression.

  • Three.js
  • React
  • Procedural creature modeling
  • Articulated animation
  • Puzzle systems
  • Save compatibility

a note on the process.

Changing the creature’s silhouette and the size of field labels improved both character and instruction. The before-and-after comparison shows why art and legibility need to be judged together.
